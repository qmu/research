# The new Gemini generation, measured — and an instrument that stops overstating itself

## Summary

Refreshes the published LLM speed and accuracy comparison to Google's newly released Gemini 3.6 Flash and 3.5 Flash-Lite, keeping each superseded model in the same table so one sweep under identical conditions can isolate what actually changed between generations. The measured answer is **mixed, not an improvement**: the new Flash is consistently slower to first token while cheaper per output token, and the new Flash-Lite is both slower to first token and materially more expensive. Reading that result also exposed two ways the surrounding article claimed more than the measurement supported, and both were fixed here rather than deferred.

## Key Changes

- Gemini 3.6 Flash and 3.5 Flash-Lite added as the current Gemini tier with web-verified prices, effort ladders, and API ids; Gemini 3.5 Flash and 3.1 Flash-Lite retained as tagged previous-generation entries paired to their successors through machine-readable registry metadata (`generation` / `supersedes` / `supersededBy`) rather than a hardcoded list
- A generational-comparison section that pairs each former→new tier, quantifies per-metric deltas over speed, accuracy, and curated cost, and derives a net verdict mechanically — a faster-but-pricier result reads as mixed, never silently netted to "improved"
- One real head-to-head sweep inside the approved ceiling: 12 of 12 configurations measured, zero errors, committed as dated frames, with the English pages, Japanese insights, and delta section all recomposed from the measured frame
- **Articles now disclose their measurement vintage.** A scoped sweep re-measures only the rows it names, so a frame routinely mixes dates; section 3 now reports the actual composition (here: 12 configurations from one date, 1 from another, 41 from a third) and states that rows of different dates are not like-for-like, instead of stamping every row with the render date
- **Directional verdicts are gated on measurement noise.** A measured metric earns a direction only when the gap between the two means clears the two models' combined run-to-run spread, which is now shown in its own column; changes inside that spread read as `indistinguishable` and are excluded from the net verdict, which reports how many were dropped

## Measured Result

| Pair | What reproduced | What did not |
| ---- | --------------- | ------------ |
| Gemini 3.5 Flash → 3.6 Flash | Slower to first token at every effort level (+21% to +29%); output price down 17% | Sustained-throughput direction — suppressed as indistinguishable at all three effort levels |
| Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite → 3.5 Flash-Lite | Slower to first token at low effort; input price up 20%, output price up 67% | Sustained-throughput direction at medium and high effort |

Cost figures are curated registry facts and carry no measurement spread. Time-to-first-token directions reproduced across two independent runs of the identical configurations, which is why they survive the gate.

## Notes

Running the identical sweep twice — the second run recovering a merge-base defect — produced replication data showing sustained throughput moving by up to 88% on the same configuration between runs. That measurement is what justified the dispersion gate, and it is also why the remaining sampling question (trial count, and whether per-effort verdicts should exist at all) is filed as follow-up work rather than answered here.

Two defects surfaced during this round are filed rather than fixed: the real comparison record is a gitignored machine-local file, so a scoped sweep on a fresh checkout can silently replace the published table instead of merging into it (graded urgent), and the generational verdicts rest on a sample the gate can only suppress, not improve.
